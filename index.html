CTC Newsletter Toolkit v1

Claude does the lists.
You write the words.

A weekly climate events newsletter for your city, without the data entry. Three Claude skills collect and format everything. You cut the list, write a short note, and send. About two hours a week.

Claude assembles the issue on Monday, and you write the words and send on Tuesday. Everything runs in your own Claude account. This page is here to hand you the prompts and explain the steps.

Set up once about 2 hours

The toolkit is three Claude skills you install in your own Claude account. You need a paid Claude plan. You do not need to know how to code. Do this once and Thursdays take care of themselves.

1 · Install the three skills

  1. In Claude, open Settings, then Capabilities, then Skills, and upload the three .skill files.
  2. Turn on code execution in the same panel. The skills need it to build your spreadsheet and document.

2 · Install the Claude Chrome extension

Some event sites hide their data from simple requests, so Claude needs to look at those pages in a real browser: yours. Search for "Claude for Chrome", install it, and sign in with the same account.

3 · Fill in your city config

Open city-config-template.md and fill it in: chapter name, timezone, what counts as local, who signs the newsletter, your footer. Save it as city-config.md. Ten minutes.

4 · Create a Claude project

Make a new project, something like "Boston Newsletter", and upload your city-config.md to it. Every week from now on happens inside that project, so Claude always knows your city and never has to be told twice.

5 · Check that the install worked

Before you leave setup, prove it to yourself. In your new project, ask:

Which newsletter skills do you have available?

Claude should name all three: ctc-source-map, ctc-harvest, and ctc-assemble. If it does, you are installed and Thursday will work. If it does not, re-upload the three .skill files, confirm code execution is on, then start a new conversation and ask again. Skills do not appear mid-conversation.

6 · Build your source registry

Your standing list of everywhere events get announced in your city. This is the most important hour you will spend, because the weekly harvest only ever finds what this list points at. Run it once now, and again every three months, because calendars die quietly.

Build the source map for [your city].

Claude researches Luma calendars, Eventbrite organisers, universities, incubators, city government, professional groups and arts venues, verifies each one is real and active, and hands you a registry. Read what it gives you. You live there and Claude does not, so if it missed the meetup everyone goes to, say so and have it add them.

Save the registry as sources.csv and upload it to your project alongside your city config. That is the last piece of setup. From here on, the weekly cycle is four short sittings.

Type it once and every prompt below fills in. Nothing is saved; this page does not store anything or connect to your Claude account.

Thursday Collect claude does this

Copy the prompt and paste it into your project. Claude sweeps every source on your registry and hands back a spreadsheet. It collects everything with no filtering, which is on purpose: collecting and judging are different jobs, and mixing them turns a short task into a long one.

Harvest this week's events for [your city].

If Claude asks to use your Chrome browser, say yes. Some sites, Eventbrite especially, hide their listings from simple requests. Claude is reading those pages, not clicking anything. Save the spreadsheet when it is done.

Friday Cut you do this

Open yesterday's spreadsheet and delete rows. No prompt for this one: it is judgment, and nobody can do it for you. How many events end up in the newsletter is decided right here, and there is no target number. A good week is however many good events your city has.

What to cut

  • Anything outside your scope rule, which is written in your city config for exactly this moment
  • Anything not really climate
  • Anything with a dead link or no date
  • If two rows are the same event from different calendars, keep the one with the better registration link and delete the other

What to flag

Mark three to six candidates for the picks. Type PICK in a spare column next to them, and simply delete the rows you are cutting. What is left in the file is the issue, and the PICK rows are what you will write about on Monday.

Monday Write claude assembles you write

Copy the prompt, paste it into your project, and attach your edited spreadsheet. Claude returns a Word document and an HTML page, both formatted for Substack.

Assemble the [your city] issue from this sheet. Rows marked PICK are my picks.

Six of the nine blocks come back finished: the title, the subtitle, both event listings, the detailed write-ups, and the footer. Every link, emoji and day header is already in place.

Three blocks come back as scaffolds with gaps marked in [square brackets]: the opening note, the picks, and the opportunities. The skill writes the structure and leaves the gaps on purpose. Those gaps are yours.

The one rule

Automate the lists. Write the words yourself. A 3,000 word issue has about 80 words of human prose, and those 80 words are why anyone subscribes.

Filling in the three gaps

Write straight into the document Claude hands back, over the [brackets]. Do not ask Claude to fill them. Generated prose reads like generated prose, and readers can tell.

  • The opening note. Two or three sentences about your city this week: the weather, something in the news, how your last event went. Then the skill's line, "Here are our picks for the week". Claude cannot know any of this, which is exactly why it left the gap.
  • The picks. Claude drafts the structure and orders the candidates. You add the asides that make it sound like a person. The archive's best read like "make calls (and friends!)" and "an infrastructure tour on wheels", and that is the difference between a newsletter and a list.
  • The opportunities. Fellowships, grants and deadlines you know about. Keep a running list somewhere; most stay open for weeks, so they carry over from issue to issue.

If the finished issue sounds like a robot, it is because these three were skipped. They are the whole point.

Tuesday Send you do this

Check, paste, read, send. About 15 minutes, and the newsletter is out.

Check first

  • Search the document for a square bracket. Nothing in brackets ships.
  • Scan the dates. Nothing in the past, and this week's events actually this week.
  • Click two or three links at random. If one is dead, check its neighbours from the same source.
  • Scan for the same event listed twice.

Then paste into Substack

  1. Open the HTML file, or the Word document, whichever you prefer.
  2. Select all, copy, and paste into a new Substack post. Formatting and links come across.
  3. Read the whole issue once, top to bottom. You are checking voice and picks, not links.
  4. Send.

Your first issue will be small, maybe five or eight events. That is what every newsletter looks like at the start. Send it anyway, on the same day, every week. A thin issue that arrives beats a good issue that does not. And if you miss a week, just send the next one, with no apology and no explanation.

When something breaks

The 9pm questions.

Harvest returned almost nothing

A source broke; sites change. Ask Claude which sources returned zero, then check those by hand. Do not assume the city went quiet.

A calendar stopped working

Mark it manual in your sources.csv and check it yourself each week.

Claude keeps asking to use my browser

Normal. Eventbrite in particular blocks simple requests. Say yes; it is reading pages, not clicking anything.

A page is asking me to prove I am human

Claude will stop and tell you. Open the page yourself and paste what you see. Claude will not solve these.

The output sounds like a robot

You skipped the writing step. The three bracketed blocks are the whole point.

Formatting broke when I pasted into Substack

Try the HTML file rather than the Word document, or the other way round.
